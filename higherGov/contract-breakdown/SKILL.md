# Contract Breakdown

Turns a government solicitation bundle into something a small business owner can understand in 10 minutes, and decide: bid or walk away.

The same steps, in the same order, every time. Do not skip steps and do not start writing a proposal here (that's a different skill).

## Step 1: Read everything in the bundle

SAM.gov opportunities usually come as a bundle: the main solicitation, attachments (statement of work, wage determinations, pricing sheets, past performance forms, the SF 1449) and sometimes amendments.

1. Read every file the user uploaded, not just the main PDF. The instructions on how to submit, and how the government will pick the winner, are often buried in an attachment.
2. Check for amendments. If there are any, later amendments replace earlier text. Always work from the latest version, and say which amendment you used.
3. If no files are uploaded, ask for them. Do not analyze a solicitation from a link or a description alone. Every requirement must come from the actual document text.

## Step 2: Plain-English Snapshot (always first)

Write this like you're explaining it to a smart friend who has never done government contracting. No jargon. Spell out every acronym the first time.

- **Who's buying and what they want done.** One normal sentence. Not "provide O&M support IAW PWS 4.2" but "they want someone to maintain and repair the heating and cooling at their building."
- **How big and how long.** Dollar value if stated, the base period and any option years.
- **The deadline.** Date, time and time zone, stated up front. Example: "Due Thursday, October 30, 2 PM Eastern." If the time zone is not stated, say so.
- **What decides the winner.** In plain words: "lowest price that meets the requirements wins" vs. "past performance matters as much as price." Quote the solicitation's own wording for this in one short line.
- **Set-aside.** Who is allowed to bid (small business, 8(a), WOSB, SDVOSB, HUBZone, or full and open).
- **Red flags.** Anything that should make them think twice: very tight deadline, a site visit that's mandatory, licenses or clearances needed, bonding, work far outside a typical small business's reach, unusual payment or penalty terms.
- **Bid or walk away?** One line with your honest read and the main reason. The user makes the final call.

## Step 3: The detailed breakdown

After the snapshot, give the fuller version for the record. Keep it factual and sourced. Never invent a requirement.

**A. Contract summary**
- Solicitation number and title, agency, contracting office
- Contract type (firm fixed price, time and materials, IDIQ, etc.)
- Period of performance and place of performance
- NAICS code and size standard
- Point of contact, and any rules about contacting the agency
- Question deadline, site visit date (if any)

**B. The work (statement of work summary)**
- What has to be done, in plain words
- Deliverables, with due dates or how often
- Performance standards (how they'll judge the work)
- Any equipment, staffing, licenses or certifications called out

**C. What they want you to submit**
- Every document and form required (for example: SF 1449, pricing sheet, past performance, capability statement, representations and certifications)
- Format rules: page limits, font, file type, file naming, how many volumes
- How to submit (email, portal, physical) and the exact deadline again

**D. How the government picks the winner**
- The evaluation method in plain words
- Each factor they score, in the order and weight the solicitation gives

## Step 4: The requirements checklist

Build a table of every "shall", "must" and "will" the contractor or the offer has to meet.

| # | Requirement (short, plain words) | Where it's from (document + page/paragraph) | Where we'll answer it in the proposal |
|---|---|---|---|

This checklist is what protects the user. A bid that misses one requirement can be thrown out before anyone reads it. The Proposal and Forms Writer skill builds the proposal from this table.

## Step 5: Hand-off

End with:
1. "Anything look off?" (solicitation language is dense; invite corrections)
2. The scope they'd likely need a subcontractor for, if any, and a suggestion to run the Sub Finder skill next.

## Output format

- Lead with the Plain-English Snapshot. Keep it to one screen.
- Then the detailed breakdown and the checklist.
- If the user asks for a file, produce a Word document; otherwise reply in chat.

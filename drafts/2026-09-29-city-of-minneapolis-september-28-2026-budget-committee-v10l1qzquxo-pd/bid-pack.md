### 2026-09-29 - City of Minneapolis - September 28, 2026 Budget Committee - V10l1qzQUXo.pdf (posted 2026-09-28)

Summary: The budget committee presentation analyzes Minneapolis’s tax-exempt property landscape and evaluates whether a payment-in-lieu-of-taxes (PILOT) program could compensate the City for municipal services provided to otherwise tax-exempt organizations.

Legislative Research and the Board of Estimate and Taxation compiled a custom spreadsheet from City Assessor data identifying property owners, estimated market values, potential 2026 property-tax liabilities, and organizational services. The underlying data remains preliminary, with final tax determinations resting with the county assessor.

Minneapolis has more than 6,000 tax-exempt properties within its boundaries, most owned by governmental entities. Excluding government-owned property, approximately 1,625 properties are held by about 770 unique organizations, including roughly 704 properties assessed at $1 million or more.

Ownership and use categories include:

- Religious organizations
- Health care providers
- Educational institutions
- Charities and social-service organizations
- Humanities and cultural facilities
- Other public-serving entities

The presenter emphasized that estimates can be affected by partial exemptions, sub-parcels, ownership-name changes, and inconsistent records. Detailed analysis of specific properties would require additional staff resources.

PILOT was described as a voluntary payment by a tax-exempt nonprofit to help offset City services such as police, fire, snow removal, and other municipal functions—not primarily as a revenue-generating tax.

Nationally, PILOT generally produces less than 1% of municipal revenue, with more than 90% of payments typically coming from colleges and hospitals. Most payments are concentrated among a small number of high-value institutions.

The University of Minnesota is a particularly significant Minneapolis property holder, with approximately $4.3 billion in land assets. Its land-grant history, state funding, independent police and land-care operations, and legal status would require special consideration in any program.

The presentation also identified objections from nonprofits, including concerns that organizations already provide public benefits, that voluntary payments could undermine tax-exempt status, and that the City may lack authority to compel payment.

Two potential program structures were reviewed:

1. **Routine annual-payment model:** A standardized request would apply across qualifying tax-exempt properties, similar to Boston’s request for 25% of estimated taxes on property valued above $15 million. Up to half of the requested amount could be satisfied through documented community benefits. Boston received 76% of its requested amount in 2024, and PILOT remained below 1% of total City revenue.

2. **Long-term contract model:** Individual memoranda of understanding would be negotiated with major institutions, producing more predictable compliance and revenue but reaching fewer organizations, as in Providence. Providence’s agreements with large universities generated cash payments plus substantial community contributions, with PILOT accounting for 2.7% of total revenue in 2025. One university, Johnson & Wales, provided nearly $7.2 million in community benefits in 2024 compared with a $600,000 City payment obligation.

No Minneapolis PILOT program was adopted in the presentation. The material was intended to inform further committee deliberation and potential staff direction.

### Description

WDFW officials confirmed that they plan to expand portable-toilet provision at Quincy Lakes following discussion of capacity at the lower trailhead. Columbia Basin Wildlife Area manager Chad Edson said there have been two portable toilets at various times, but he believed only one was currently present. Agency staff said they could consider adding more and discussed configurations including one at the designated camping area and one at the trailhead, or two at the trailhead and one at the camping area. Edson concluded, “we'll need to expand on that. That's the plan.”

The opportunity could involve additional portable-toilet rentals and associated pumping and servicing. Officials described relying on vendors' usage records and recommendations to determine capacity needs. During discussion of the existing servicing arrangement, Edson identified “Apple pumping”; this was a reference to the current service provider, not an announced award or selection for the expansion.

No final quantity, rental duration, budget, or implementation date was stated. This potential expansion at accessible trailhead and designated camping locations is separate from the larger, conditional road-and-vault-toilet project proposed to support backpack camping near the lakes.

### 2026-10-07 - Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife - Public Meeting on proposed Quincy Lakes camping closure - zV_yLPXY41M.pdf (posted 2026-10-06)

Summary: The public meeting addresses WDFW’s proposed prohibition on camping within the Quincy Lakes unit of the Columbia Basin Wildlife Area while allowing hiking and other noncamping recreation to continue.

The proposed closure area lies between the Columbia River and main Quincy Lakes Road, extending from the Ancient Lakes trailhead south to Sunland Estates Road, and excludes the Frenchman climbing area.

WDFW attributes the proposal primarily to habitat damage from sagebrush removal, wildfire risks from campfires—including reported fires during prohibited periods—and sanitation problems associated with dispersed shoreline camping more than a mile from the trailhead portable toilet.

The agency noted that the property was acquired in 1960 as part of a 7,200-acre acquisition funded through Pittman-Robertson federal excise-tax revenues and a 50-percent state match from wildlife-related funds.

WDFW explained that the Columbia Basin Wildlife Area has five staff members, only two of whom are realistically available for field work across approximately 190,000 acres, while three enforcement officers cover three counties.

Its operations and maintenance budget is approximately $4 per acre and is already committed to weed control, signage, dumping, abandoned vehicles, and other priorities.

The agency stated that sustainable future camping remains possible but would require:

- A defined camping boundary or specific designated campsites
- Controls on vehicle parking and spillover
- A year-round campfire ban
- Improved enforcement access
- An access road
- A vault toilet or additional portable toilets

WDFW estimated the necessary infrastructure and compliance work at approximately $200,000–$250,000 and said it would likely need to pursue a Recreation and Conservation Office state-lands development grant.

The next application cycle opens in 2028, with funding potentially available after July 1, 2029, subject to grant approval and operations-and-maintenance funding.

The meeting also reviewed public participation procedures.

General comments, ideas, and contact information for possible future planning groups may be submitted through WDFW’s public-input process, while comments on environmental impacts or the environmental analysis should be submitted through the SEPA process.

Comments were scheduled to remain open through October 30, 2026.

During discussion, participants proposed seasonal camping, additional toilets, and stewardship coordination, but WDFW emphasized that sanitation facilities would still be necessary if backpacking camping continues.

WDFW clarified that the anticipated 2027 action would prohibit camping within the defined footprint, not close the area to hiking, and acknowledged that litter and general user stewardship have been comparatively strong.
